# How the Kazi digitizer works

The page at `/digitizer` turns a picture (a logo, artwork, a photograph) into a machine embroidery file (DST, PES, EXP, JEF, VP3, XXX), entirely in the visitor's browser. Nothing is uploaded: the picture, the tracing and the stitching all run on the visitor's computer. The one large download is the person-finder model, for photos only (about 35 MB: a 16 MB person model, a 4 MB face model and a 15 MB wasm file, kept by the browser afterwards, see 5.6 and 5.8). In a network check on 2026-11-12 the only request made while converting was the host's own `/cdn-cgi/rum` beacon.

This file describes the code as it stands after the 2026-11-13 edge fix (local commit `40c71ac`) and the 2026-11-14 face work (5.6 and 5.8; neither pushed when this was written). Paths are relative to the Next.js project folder `app/` unless they start with `app/` or `docs/` (those are from the repository root); the route file is `app/app/digitizer/page.js`. The deepest reference for the tracer and the stitch engine is `app/lib/embroidery/README.md` (long, dated paragraphs with the measurements behind each rule). This file is the map: what happens, in what order, with which numbers, and where in the code.

**Nothing in the digitizer has been sewn on a real machine.** Every quality statement below is measured on screen (stitch counts, colour error against the source, drawings of the stitch file), not on cloth.

---

## 1. The one-paragraph version

The page decodes the picture, a worker **traces** it into flat colour regions (a palette plus smooth polygons), the page **converts** those regions into design objects (one per shape, with a colour, rows angle and an optional edge line), a second worker's **stitch engine** turns every object into stitches (tatami fill, satin columns, bean lines, underlay, travel, trims), and a **writer** encodes the stitches as a machine file. The person edits objects in a canvas editor in between; any change re-runs the stitch engine on the design.

```
picture file
  -> loadPicture / extractImage            (page: decode, crop, shrink to at most 1600 px for tracing)
  -> [person finder + face finder]         (photos only: MediaPipe region map and face landmarks, own worker)
  -> traceImage                            (worker A: palette + polygons + fine lines)       lib/embroidery/trace/
  -> objectsFromTrace + applyLook          (worker A: shapes -> design objects)               lib/digitizer/convert.js, look.js
  -> generate                              (worker B: objects -> stitches)                    lib/embroidery/stitch/
  -> writePattern                          (page: DST/PES/... bytes, on demand)               lib/embroidery/formats/
```

Units, everywhere: **design space is millimetres, origin at the hoop centre, +x right, +y DOWN**; a **pattern** (what files hold) is integer **0.1 mm** units, absolute positions, +y down (the DST writer flips y itself); angles are degrees, 0 along +x, positive clockwise on screen; image pixels are the picture's own grid and `mmPerPx` converts to millimetres.

## 3. What the visitor does (the editor)

Three steps along the top of the canvas:

1. **Picture**: size (width in mm), crop, how strongly the picture shows behind the stitches ("Show"), how it is turned into shapes (picture type Auto/Logo/Artwork/Photo; colours Auto, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 8, 10 or 12; the Detail slider from 0 to 1 in steps of 0.05, labelled Simple under 0.34, Balanced under 0.67, Detailed under 0.95 and Finest above, default 0.75; "Sharper edges", cloth colour, "Leave out the background", "Find the person in a photo", "Use the nearest thread colours", "Fine details on top", "Thicker fine lines", "Convert again"), the colour list (each colour can be ticked on or off), and "Trace by hand" (below). Picture files: PNG, JPEG, WebP, GIF, BMP or SVG (`lib/digitizer/image.js`; an SVG is drawn big so its edges are crisp); a picture is loaded up to 3200 px on its long side and the tracer is given at most 1600 px (`extractImage`).
2. **Design**: select/move/resize/rotate shapes, rectangle/ellipse/text tools, per-object fill and outline (type, angle, spacing, pull compensation, underlay, stroke), colours, lock/hide, undo/redo.
3. **Stitches**: live stitch preview and playback (Slow 800, Normal 3000, Fast 12000 stitches a second), the numbers (stitches, colours, size, time estimate), fabric preset, export buttons, a printable production sheet.

Header numbers (`lib/digitizer/stats.js`, read straight from the flat stitch array): stitches, colours (different needles, not blocks), jumps, trims, colour changes, size in mm, thread length in metres, and a time estimate of stitches / 700 a minute plus 0.5 min per colour change, 0.06 min per trim and 0.015 min per jump.

**Tracing by hand** (`lib/digitizer/trace.js`, `TraceBar.jsx`): the person clicks points round a shape on the picture. A point is `[x, y, smooth]` in mm; `smooth` 0 is a corner, 1 a curve point. Between two corners the outline is a straight line; any other piece is a cubic curve whose handles run along the line from the point before to the point after, a third of the piece long, so a curve never swings wider than the points that hold it. A shape under 0.2 mm² is taken for a slip of the finger and dropped (`MIN_TRACE_AREA_MM2`). Hand-traced shapes are never touched by the angle or edge look.

**The face note and "Crop to the face"** (Picture panel, `PicturePanel.jsx`; only when the face finder found a face in a person it was asked about): a line says how wide the face and one eye will be on the cloth ("The face is about 14 mm wide, so each eye is about 2.5 mm"), the face is measured in the picture's own pixels and multiplied by the picture's millimetres a pixel NOW, so the note stays true after a resize, a crop, an undo or a redo; under `FACE_EYE_MIN_MM` (2 mm) it is a warning ("Eyes that small sew as dark marks, not as eyes"). "Crop to the face" (`cropToFace` in `useDigitizer.js`) crops to the head and shoulders round the face (`faceCropBox`: about 2.5 face-widths wide, the hair above, the neck and shoulders below, kept inside the picture; no button when that would not be much smaller than the picture), makes the result fill the hoop, and converts again. In the real page the astronaut goes from a face 14 mm wide (eyes 2.5 mm) to one 34 mm wide (eyes 6.2 mm) in about a second.

**Hoops** (`HOOPS` in `lib/digitizer/model.js`): Cap 150x60, Left chest 100x100 (default), 120x120, Small 130x180, 150x150, 180x180, Medium 200x200, 240x240, Large 260x260, 300x300, Back 300x350, 350x350, 400x400, Biggest 400x450.

**Exports** (`lib/digitizer/formatsInfo.js`): DST (most industrial machines), PES (Brother/Babylock), EXP (Melco/Bernina), JEF (Janome), VP3 (Husqvarna Viking/Pfaff), XXX (Singer/Compucon); "Save all six" exports them together. Before writing, the stitches are centred on the hoop centre (the machine zero). A saved design is JSON (`serializeDesign`); it does **not** keep the source picture, so re-opening loses the Picture step (stitches can differ by about 1 in 8,400).

**Autosave and storage**: a design is autosaved to `localStorage` 1.5 s after the last change (`kazi.digitizer.autosave`), plus `kazi.digitizer.settings` (the picture settings) and `kazi.digitizer.hoop`. If the browser refuses the write, the old autosave is dropped and the person is told once. Nothing leaves the browser. **Gap to fix:** the cookies page (`components/CookiePolicyPage.jsx`) says it lists exactly what the site stores in the browser (the bag, the Atelier handoff) and that the site runs no analytics. It does not list these three keys, and the 2026-11-12 network check saw the host's `/cdn-cgi/rum` beacon on this page (it may be a Cloudflare dashboard setting; not re-checked since).

**Text tool** (`lib/digitizer/text.js`): the browser draws the text on a canvas (`drawText`: 14 px per mm, at most 4096 px on the long side; the size setting is the height of a capital, taken as 0.72 of the em) in one of nine fixed font stacks (Sans = Arial, Sans heavy = Arial Black, Condensed = Impact, Humanist = Trebuchet MS / Segoe UI, Serif = Georgia, Times, Typewriter = Courier New, Script = Brush Script MT / Segoe Script, Rounded = Comic Sans MS / Chalkboard SE), so what is drawn depends on the fonts the visitor's device has. With no extra letter spacing a line is drawn whole, so the browser kerns it and joins scripts such as Devanagari itself; with spacing it is drawn one grapheme (a letter with its marks, found with `Intl.Segmenter`) at a time. The same tracer then vectorises the glyph pixels (`useDigitizer.js`: colours 2, `minFeatureMm 0.15`, `minAreaMm2 0.05`, `simplifyMm 0.03`, background "auto"). The words stay on the object (`obj.text`), so editing a word redraws and re-traces it. Typed lettering is object `kind: "text"` and gets the looser satin limit (see 7.2).

**Keyboard**: shortcuts (for example Ctrl/Cmd+A selects every unlocked object, Ctrl/Cmd+D duplicates the selection) and Space-to-pan act only while focus or the pointer is inside the editor, because this page scrolls and window-wide shortcuts once hijacked Ctrl+A / Ctrl+D. `data-lenis-prevent` is set on the editor so the site's smooth scroll leaves the wheel alone over the canvas (the wheel zooms it). The floating WhatsApp button hides while the editor is on screen below 1560 px (`html[data-digitizer-open]`).

## 4. Workers, jobs and limits

Two Web Workers (`engineClient.js`):

- **generate worker**: makes stitches (`engineCore.run("generate")`).
- **other worker**: everything else (`trace`, `shapes`, `quantize`, `release`).

Rules: jobs go in as promises; for one `key` only one job is in flight and only the newest waits (dragging a slider costs one job in flight plus the last); a stale answer is discarded; **a job over 2 minutes is given up on** and its worker is restarted (the other worker is untouched); with no Worker (tests, very old browsers) the same functions run on the page's thread. Outlines travel as typed arrays (`packRings.js`) and are handed over, not copied. The tracer's answer can stay inside the worker under a `keep` token (a big answer is tens of thousands of points; sending it froze the page): the page gets a summary, and `shapes` later turns the kept answer into objects for whichever colours are switched on. Up to 4 answers are kept.

Guard rails in the engine: every shape number is clamped to a range the engine can work with; a ring with a NaN or a point more than 5000 mm from the hoop centre is dropped with a warning; a design that would need more than 1,500,000 stitches is refused before any work.

**Turbopack trap**: Next's bundler runs workers as *classic* workers whatever `type` is passed, so MediaPipe must use `vision_wasm_internal.*` (classic), not `..._module_internal.*` (that threw "Cannot use 'import.meta' outside a module"). The person finder fails silently by design (the picture is then traced as a plain photo), so check the worker console when debugging.

## 5. Stage 1: reading the picture (`lib/embroidery/trace/`)

`traceImage(image, opts)` returns `{ width, height, palette, shapes, order, stats }` (plus `details` and `hairlines` when asked). Coordinates are pixels of the original picture (floats, +y down). A **shape** is one connected piece of one colour: `contours[0]` is the outer boundary (clockwise on screen), later contours are holes. **Touching regions of different colours share exactly the same boundary points**, so there are no gaps or overlaps (and "which shape sits in whose hole" can be found by matching points). Everything is deterministic.

### 5.1 Kind of picture (`photo.js`)

Found from how well 16 colours describe the picture's *flat* pixels on a lightly blurred copy: **logo** (flat colours), **artwork** (soft shading) or **photo**. An engraving or woodcut (black ink on paper) has lines closer together than a thread can follow, so it is read as a photograph when it has strong local contrast at half its pixels, at least 500 px on its long side and up to 120 mm wide (a bigger design may follow the lines). The person can force Logo/Artwork/Photo.

### 5.2 Preparation and colours (`prep.js`, `quantize.js`, `color.js`)

Anti-alias and JPEG clean-up, then Lab k-means to a palette of up to 12 colours (a machine head's needles; "Auto" uses only as many as the picture needs). Colours of artwork and photographs are merged harder (delta-E 8 and 6, against 11 for a logo). Colours are the picture's own; the chart name is only a label unless "nearest thread" is on. A small picture that must be enlarged for tracing finds its colours on its own pixels, so the colour count does not change with the size it is sewn at. The working grid is about 0.15 mm for photographs and never finer than 0.06 mm a pixel; a picture whose pixels would be bigger than 0.4 mm once stitched is enlarged smoothly to about 0.2 mm a pixel. A photograph is first flattened with a Kuwahara filter (the 3.5 % of pixels it changed most are put back: lashes, whiskers, twigs). `missingColours` and `missingNeutral` rescue ink that k-means swallowed (colours that are only thin lines; a coherent light grey among a full palette, replacing one slot rather than adding a needle).

### 5.3 Background (`frame.js`, rules in the header of `trace/index.js`)

- Real transparency (at least 0.05 % of pixels under 40 % alpha): the see-through pixels are the background and nothing else.
- Otherwise the palette colour covering at least 55 % of the picture's outer ring is the background, but only the part **connected to the edge** (flood fill): white letters inside a navy disc, the counter of an O, are not background (they become an "enclosed background" palette entry that is stitched or not like any colour).
- A thin 1 to 10 px frame of one colour round the whole picture is painted over (`removeFrame`) so it is not mistaken for the background.
- A dark or strongly coloured background sets the **cloth colour** to it (the picture was made for that garment); a light one leaves the default cream `#f4f1ea`.
- **"Leave out the background" unticked means nothing is left out (2026-11-14).** `background: "none"` makes the tracer mark no palette entry as background, keep a frame round the picture (`trimFrame: false`) and say so (`stats.backgroundOff`); `convert.js` then reads the cloth match as 0 for every kind of picture (`clothMatchOf`), so the white of a logo's page, of its letters' counters and of a pale photograph is stitched in white like any other colour, and `contentBox` takes the whole picture. Before, the box only stopped the *tracer* leaving out the page; the converter still left out every colour like the cloth ("the cloth shows through"), which was leaving out the background by another door. The person finder is not asked either (it needs the background to cut). Tested in `lib/digitizer/selftest.mjs` and on logos, a seal, a crest and a portrait (the Nike swoosh 1 thread / 1,183 stitches becomes 2 threads / 4,410: the swoosh and its white page).

### 5.4 Thin strokes are kept (`labels.js`)

Lettering and hairlines of the minor colours are *widened* to about 0.5 mm along their own middle lines (`thickenMm`, 0.8 at the lowest Detail, 0.6 Balanced, 0.5 default, 0.45 Finest), because a stroke thinner than a thread cannot be sewn. Widened pixels are marked so they do not count when a colour is measured. In artwork and photographs only long thin pieces count. Pieces under a size limit are dropped, but the limit scales with contrast (a bright petal on dark leaves stays, a patch of sky a shade off goes); a piece the size of a thread with strongly different colours on both sides (a vein, a twig, a lash) is kept at 0.5 mm (up to a 60 mm design; 0.3 mm from 100 mm).

### 5.5 Hairlines, thin lines, fine details (`hairlines.js`, `photo.js`, `details.js`, `fine.js`)

For artwork and photographs only (a logo is traced exactly):

- **Hairlines** (`hairlines.js`): pieces at most 0.36 mm across and at least 2 mm long (chains at least 0.8 mm) are taken out of the label map *before* widening and returned as centre lines (thinning, followed end to end, simplified to 0.5 px), at most 150 chains and 12 mm of line per cm². They are sewn as single bean lines (the tiger's whiskers are separate lines, not one filled blob), on top, in a thread of the design within 24 CIELAB of the colour the line has in the picture (the darkest or lightest half of its pixels, not the palette colour it was drawn in) and at least 8 CIELAB different from what lies under it; never a new colour, at most two threads for all of them.
- **Thin lines** (`photo.js`): lashes, whiskers, veins found from the luminance of the unflattened picture (a closing fills a dark line, an opening removes a light one); a piece counts if it stands out by more than 40 of 255 and is at least 1.2 mm long; all lines together are held to the strongest 3 % of the picture (falling to 1.2 % at 250 mm).
- **Fine details** (`details.js`): ridges of lightness at three scales from Gaussian second derivatives with a sub-pixel centre, linked into polylines, scored by how much their centre differs from both sides in CIELAB, kept within a millimetres-of-line budget per cm² (more at higher Detail); compact blobs of 0.45 to 8 mm² become dots. `fine.js` drops what the colour layer already carries.
- In the design these become `layer: "detail"` objects: open lines (`open: true`, bean 0.45 mm, or a 0.7 mm narrow satin with "Thicker fine lines") and dots, sewn **last**, in the nearest existing thread (within 18 CIELAB); when none is near and the machine has needles to spare, up to two new threads are added (at most three threads for all the details, so at most three more colour changes at the end); a detail that would not show on what lies under it (under 14 CIELAB different) is left out. Their stitches are kept under 30 % of the colour layer's, and their separate pieces under a fifth of its shapes (at least 12).

A picture is held to about 2500 shapes (up to 2.4 times that from a 240 mm design) by raising the limits.

### 5.6 Person finder (`segment.js`, `segmentClient.js`, `segment.worker.js`, `regions.js`, `regionPalette.js`)

Only when the first reading says "photo" and the person has not unticked "Find the person in a photo" or "Leave out the background". Google's MediaPipe selfie-multiclass model (Apache-2.0; 16 MB tflite plus 14 MB wasm in `public/mediapipe/`, downloaded the first time and cached by the browser) gives a 256 px class map (background, hair, body skin, face skin, clothes, other), sharpened to the picture's edges with a colour-guided filter. A picture with no face (biggest face-skin piece under 1.5 % of it, or low confidence) is traced as an ordinary photo. With a map the tracer cuts the background out, builds a **palette per region** (each region gets the colours it earns, see below), classifies each pixel only among its own region's colours, keeps one colour back for the details' own thread, and reads the Detail slider half a step higher. The same job then asks the face finder (5.8) where the eyes, brows and lips are.

**How the colours are shared out (changed 2026-11-14).** Each region first gets one colour, the face (class 3) gets 3 and its eyes (class 6, when the face finder found them) 2 before anything is shared by gain, and the rest go where one more colour takes away the most weighted error, counted by the region's size to the power 0.7 (it was the plain pixel count, power 1). Before, a big plain region won every colour: the astronaut's suit and helmet got 9 colours and his face one (a flat patch with a mouth line, no eyes, eye contrast 0.08 of the source's). The numbers are `REGION_AREA_POWER` and `REGION_MIN_COLORS` in `regionPalette.js` (and the trace options `regionAreaPower`, `regionMinColors`, `regionFeatureExtra`); a palette under 8 colours is the person's own choice and gets no minimums. What it cost and what it bought, measured on 19 portraits (16 of the 55-picture Wikimedia set, tuned on one half and checked on the other, and 3 older ones): the whole person +0.1 dE and 1.4 % fewer stitches, the astronaut's face correlation 0.49 to 0.89 and his eye contrast 0.08 to 0.8; pictures that are not people (40 of the 55) came out byte for byte the same.

**What was wrong, and what was not (found 2026-11-13, corrected 2026-11-14).** The two portraits the engine lost to DADE were first put down to "Leave out the background" blanking the face and dropping black hair. Only the face was the engine's fault (the colour shares above). The woman's "missing hair" was my own comparison picture: the tool had set the cloth to the photo's near-black backdrop (`#0e0e0e`), so her black hair is meant to be the cloth, and the picture was drawn on cream cloth. When judging a result, draw it on the cloth the tool chose (`stats.cloth` in the test harness, the page shows it).

### 5.7 Outlines (`contours.js`, `simplify.js`, `repair.js`)

The label map (one palette index per pixel) becomes outlines like this. The pixel corners are a lattice, and the line between two pixels with different labels is a *crack*, a unit step between two lattice points. Cracks join into **chains** (a run between two junctions, a junction being a point where three or four cracks meet; or a closed loop with no junction), and chains make up the **loops** that bound a region (outer boundaries clockwise on screen, holes anticlockwise). A chain has one region on each side and is smoothed **once**, so both regions use the very same points: that is why neighbours never leave a gap or overlap. The line goes through the middle of its cracks, nudged along the pixel pair to where the real edge is (the soft shares of an anti-aliased pixel say where), which removes the pixel staircase.

`simplify.js` then cleans each chain: resample it evenly and find the corners (sharp turns within a pixel or two); smooth the stretches between corners with a Taubin filter (smooths without shrinking curves); put each corner exactly where the two straight sides either side of it meet, and each junction where the lines of all its chains meet (this is what keeps square corners square); drop the points that do not matter (Ramer-Douglas-Peucker); draw a smooth cubic curve through the points left and write it out as a polyline within a small tolerance of the curve. A straight side comes out as two points, a circle as a modest number of points, every corner is a point. Points are stored as flat `Float64Array`s of the working grid's pixels.

`repair.js` is a last resort for an outline that crosses itself (two letters of one colour touching at a point with a V-shaped gap, the corner of a very acute wedge): it pulls the offending point back along its own bisector, never moves a junction, and works on shared chain points so both regions still see identical points.

### 5.8 Faces (`faceFinder.js`, `faces.js`; added 2026-11-14)

MediaPipe's **Face Landmarker** (Apache-2.0 project; a 3.8 MB `face_landmarker.task` in `public/mediapipe/`, listed in `NOTICE.txt`) gives 478 points a face (outline, eyes with their lids, irises, brows, lips). `faceFinder.js` runs it in the person finder's worker, on the same MediaPipe runtime (no second wasm), **only after the person model has called the picture a person**, so a logo or a flower never loads it; it never throws (no file, no wasm: `[]` and the picture is read as before). The worker hands the points back as flat `Float32Array`s (478 x 3). The face landmarker's own face detector runs at a low resolution, so the small faces of a crowd, a group or a full-length figure are not found (none of the 4 test pictures of that kind): too small to sew anyway.

`faces.js` (pure, no DOM, tested under node with a saved face in `selftest.mjs`):
- `faceParts(landmarks, W, H)`: the eyes, brows, irises, lips and outline in the picture's pixels, the face's width and height, an eye's width and the distance between the eyes.
- `plausibleFace(parts)`: the landmarker fits a face onto a cat or a toy, so the proportions are checked (eyes alike, the mouth about one eye-distance under them and on the chin's side, a face about as wide as it is long). A cat passes this check (its proportions are human-like): what keeps cats and dogs out is that the features are only used on a picture the person model calls a person (the dog photo is called a person but has no landmarks; the cat is not called one).
- `paintFeatureClasses(classes, W, H, faces, { mmPerPx })`: paints class 6 (`FACE_REGION.FEATURES`: eyes with lids and irises, and brows) and class 7 (`FACE_REGION.LIPS`) into the region map, over the person only, grown by 0.45, 0.35 and 0.3 mm. The page does this for each trace at that trace's size (`personAt` in `useDigitizer.js`), because a dilation in millimetres is a different number of pixels at each size. The tracer then finds the dark of the iris, the white round it and the red of the lips from the picture's own pixels: the landmarks say WHERE, the picture says WHAT (lines drawn on the landmarks would give everyone the same face).
- `faceMeasures(parts, mmPerPx)`: the face's width and an eye's width on the cloth. Sewn at eight sizes in real scale (the astronaut, faces 4.9 to 26 mm wide): an eye under 1.4 mm is a smudge or nothing, at 1.7 mm two dark marks, at 2.3 mm a lid line and an iris, from about 3 mm an eye with its white. `FACE_EYE_MIN_MM` (2 mm) is where the page warns.
- `faceCropBox`, `mainFaces`: the head-and-shoulders crop for "Crop to the face", and the faces that count (the biggest plausible one and any at least a third as wide).

The tracer side (`regions.js`, `regionPalette.js`): classes 6 and 7 are live from 60 work pixels (a face's features are under the 1 %-of-the-person limit the other classes need), may add 3 colours on top of the palette that mostly merge into the face's and the hair's, and after the merging the palette is never more than was asked for. Weights 3 and 2 for the two classes, detail weights 6 and 3.

**The whites of a face: eye whites and teeth (added 2026-11-14, after "it doesn't detect eyeballs well, teeth, white stuff").** The palette of each region is found from flat patches of that region, and an eye white or a row of teeth is never a flat patch: a few blurred pixels between the lid and the lips. So no thread was ever made for them: the teeth came out the colour of the lips or skin, the eye whites a dark disc in skin (measured on 15 faces: the bright part of the teeth rendered at L 37 where the source had 49, the eye white at L 34 against 55; in the render a smile had no teeth). The person model does not call them background on photographs (on every real portrait of the test set it says face skin; only on a pencil drawing and a cartoon, which the page would not send to it, it said background), so nothing was "cut out": they were just never given a white. Now:
- `featureAnchors(image, faces)` measures them where the landmarks say they are: the **eye white** inside the eye outline, outside the iris grown by 20 % (the lightest 40 % of the pixels that are bright, `L >= 50`, and hardly coloured, chroma `<= 22`, median colour), kept only when it differs from the skin round the eye by 10 CIELAB or more; the **teeth** inside the opening of a mouth that is open (the lips apart in the middle by at least 6 % of the mouth's width: a mouth at rest is 0 to 2 %, a smile with teeth 10 to 30 %), the lightest 60 % of the bright (`L >= 50`), uncoloured (chroma `<= 24`) pixels. Yellow or brown teeth are not "white": nothing is measured (the palette has that colour already). It runs in the person finder's worker, where the picture's pixels are, and returns `[{ kind, rgb, classes, px }]` (`anchors` of `findPerson`, `img.person.anchors`, trace option `regionAnchors`, at most four).
- Two more **zone** classes in the region map: 8 = the white of the eye (inside the outline, outside the iris), 9 = the opening of an open mouth. A zone has **no palette of its own**: the palette is built exactly as if the zones were the eyes (6) and the lips (7) they lie in, and a zone only says *where a measured white may be used* (`ZONE_PARENT` in `regions.js`; whatever else reads classes, like the details' region in `fine.js`, reads a zone as its parent). Without a measured white for a zone it changes nothing at all (checked: 0 of 26 faces differ with anchors off).
- In `buildRegionalPalette` an anchor is an extra palette entry with the zone's mask bit, **paid for in the shares** (the budget is smaller by one per anchor, so a low-gain colour of the clothes goes, not the closest pair of colours anywhere, which cost Chawla her lips' pink when the anchors were added afterwards), **kept exactly as measured** when merged with a colour that is alike (and never the colour merged away to stay under the needles), and not averaged back by `clusterMeans` (`pal2.fixed`: the mean of the pixels it ends up covering is the grey of the gaps between teeth, `#bcbcbc`, where the teeth are `#ede6df`). `classifyByRegion` lets a zone use the colours of its parent class plus its white, so a shine on a lip or a brow, as white as the teeth, is not sewn in the white (tested with a palette made by hand).
- A safeguard in `paintFeatureClasses`: what the landmarks put inside an eye or the open mouth is taken back from the background (class 0) when the face is mostly person already (at least 60 % of its outline), as the white of the eye (8) or the iris (6) and the open mouth (9) or the lips (7); a face on a poster behind the person takes nothing back. (It changed no pixel of the 15 portraits of the test set that went through the person path; it is there for the pictures where the person finder does call a mouth background.)
- Measured on the 26 faces of the 73 test pictures: 10 change (the ones with a measurable white), mean face colour error 8.33 to 8.34, face luminance correlation unchanged, eye contrast 0.904 to 0.904, eye structure correlation 0.704 to 0.691 (three faces, the elder, the glasses and the girl with the blue eyes, account for it: their eyes are closed, behind glasses or shaded, so the metric is noise there). What it buys is not in those numbers: the smile of the NASA portrait has white teeth and pink lips, the boy of Madagascar has the whites of his eyes and his teeth, the man with the gap teeth his teeth. A mouth open by a millimetre shows a strip of white a millimetre high: the landmarks say that is all there is.

Result on the 18 earlier test pictures and the 55 new ones (`lib/embroidery/README.md`, "Faces first"): the astronaut gets shading, brows, a mouth and blue irises where he had a flat patch; the Pearl painting gets lids and two-part lips; larger faces (20 to 50 mm) change little on the metrics and look a little cleaner (3 to 4 more tones, pink lips); 40 of the 55 pictures are unchanged. Known weaknesses: black-and-white portraits are read as ARTWORK (a good fit of 16 greys), so the person finder and the face finder are never asked for them and the senator comes out as four threads; a person found only in a crowd is not found.

## 6. Stage 2: from shapes to design objects (`lib/digitizer/convert.js`, `look.js`)

`objectsFromTrace(traced, { originX, originY, mmPerPx, skip, include, fabric, snap, before, maxColors, detailThick })` makes one object per shape:

```js
{ id, name, kind, visible, locked, rings (centred), transform {x,y,sx,sy,rot},
  fill:   { type: "auto"|"tatami"|"satin"|"none", color, angle, spacing, stitchLen, stagger, pullComp, inset, underlay },
  stroke: { type: "none"|"run"|"bean"|"satin", color, width, len, position, spacing, pullComp, inset, dev },
  source: "image" | "hand" | ..., pal (palette entry), open?, layer? }
```

Key rules:

- **Cloth colour** (`CLOTH_MATCH` 14 CIELAB): a picture colour like the cloth is not stitched where it is wide (cloth shows through). Its shapes narrower than 2.5 mm (`CLOTH_FINE_MM`) are stitched in their own colour where they are ink (in a hole of a shape at least 4 mm wide, `CLOTH_FIELD_MM`), so white lettering on a navy badge works; counters (inside an a, e, B) and a white outline round the whole design stay cloth. The palette entry then reads "fine parts only". For photos the match is 7 and never against the default cream (hair and shirts would vanish).
  **Practical trap: on the default cream cloth, large white areas of a logo are not stitched.** Set the cloth colour to the real garment (or any non-white) to stitch white.
- **Bleed**: every shape reaches 0.4 mm under the shapes sewn after it (`bleedUnder`), so seams show thread, never cloth; a shape inside the hole of a later border does not grow.
- **Order**: objects follow the tracer's sewing order (a later shape lies over an earlier one, which is why each shape bleeds under the ones after it); the engine then groups colours only where nothing overlaps (7.1).
- **Look** (`look.js`): two settings, both on by default, applied to objects made from the picture only (hand-set angles, hand traces and text are never touched):
  - *Angles* (`angles.js`): rows run along the long axis of a shape that is at least 1.6 times longer than wide; touching shapes take different angles from a set of eight so neighbours differ by 40 degrees or more; angles are computed over every shape together so switching a colour off and on never changes a stayer's angle (6 % fewer stitches on 25 pictures).
  - *Edges* (`edges.js`): see 6.1.
- `changedColours` is the single function that says which colours a change (on/off or a cloth change) touched; `objectsFromTrace`, `mergeTraceObjects` and the worker all use it.

### 6.1 The edge line ("Sharper edges", `edges.js` and `stitch/outline.js`)

A fill's rows end at the edge, and a row end is round and 0.4 mm wide, so a straight edge sewn at 45 degrees shows teeth about a third of a millimetre deep, and the sewn shape comes out about 0.17 mm fatter than the traced one. A thin running-stitch line just inside the edge (thread 0.4 mm, stitches 3 mm, middle 0.2 mm inside the true edge) with the fill's rows stopping 0.3 mm short under it gives the edge back (sewn shapes then 0.05 mm fatter).

Current rule (changed 2026-11-13, commit `40c71ac`): flat shapes of kind logo or artwork (never photos), mean width at least **1.6 mm** and area at least **4 mm²** (was 3 mm and 10 mm²); a long stroke at least 3.5 times longer than wide and under 5 mm wide is left alone (the engine may sew it as satin) **only if it has no holes** (a bar with holes in it is a fill).

**Curve-following stitches** (same change): running and bean lines place the needle holes where a stitch would cut across the curve by more than `dev` (0.08 mm for outlines, `lineDev` 0.07 for thin lines), and keep long stitches on straight edges (`densify` + `curveRun` in `stitch/path.js`). A fixed 3 mm stitch round a 4 mm disc is a hexagon (the chord sags 0.5 mm). Cost: about 4.8 % more stitches over 24 test pictures, trims unchanged.

## 7. Stage 3: stitches (`lib/embroidery/stitch/engine.js` and friends)

`generate(design, options)` returns `{ pattern, warnings, info, runs, ms }`. `pattern = { name, threads: [{r,g,b,...}], stitches: [[x,y,cmd]] }` with `CMD = { STITCH 0, JUMP 1, TRIM 2, COLOR_CHANGE 3, STOP 4, END 5 }`, same meaning as pyembroidery. N colour blocks have N threads and N-1 colour changes. `info` says, per piece, what was chosen and why (for example `satin refused: needle holes 2.4 mm apart on an edge (up to 0.8)`).

### 7.1 Objects to items and order

An object is up to two items: its **fill** and its **stroke** (each with its own colour). Open objects (`open: true`, the fine lines) are line items. The order of objects is the design's order; it is changed only to save colour changes **where nothing overlaps**: which shapes lie over which is found by drawing every shape into one 0.1 mm grid (`overlapPairs`; `overlap.js` on the shapes' own grid for designs too wide), because cutting every pair with the polygon engine took minutes on a portrait. Detail items are held back until everything before them is sewn. Within a colour the nearest piece goes next, then untangled with 2-opt.

### 7.2 The choice for each island of a fill (`objectRuns`)

For `fill.type = "auto"`, each island (outer ring plus holes) is sewn by exactly one of:

1. **Too small**: under `minIsland` 0.3 mm² it is dropped with a (single, merged) warning.
2. **A thin line** (`meanW < lineMaxWidth` 0.7 mm for shapes from a picture, 0.58 for hand shapes; at least `lineRatio` 2.5 times longer than wide; `lineHoles` any): one **bean line** along the middle of its skeleton (`strokeCenterlines`): each stretch (`lineLen` 1.4 mm, curve-following since 2026-11-13) forward, back, forward, about 0.5 mm wide; branches joined where they meet. This is how script hairlines and small lettering are sewn. (`lineImpl: "runs"`; "island" is the alternative using `lineIsland`, scored the same and costs 1.7 % more stitches.)
3. **Satin**: `satinIsland` along the skeleton: a column as wide as the stroke at each point (a ray to each edge, kept within reach of the local half width), stations `spacing` 0.3 mm apart, an extra station at each real corner with its own miter, strokes running straight through a junction merged, free ends run on to the tip. Underlay by width (none under 1 mm, centre run under 3.2, edge runs under 5.5, both above). The satin is **checked before it is kept**; "auto" accepts it only if all hold: widest part at most `autoSatinWidth` 5 mm, at least `autoSatinRatio` 3.5 times longer than wide, no part over `autoSatinSteady` 2.2 times the average width, coverage at least `satinCoverage` 0.85 (thread taken as 0.45 mm), spill past the edge at most 0.15, no more than `satinExcess` 1.7 times the thread the shape needs, no more than 0.2 of it piled four threads deep, and needle holes on one edge never further apart than `autoSatinGap` **0.8 mm** (1.3 for satin asked for by hand, 2 mm `textSatinGap` for typed lettering). Parts thinner than `satinMinStroke` 0.4 mm are left out of a satin layout.
4. **Tatami fill** (otherwise): see 7.3, with thin arms cut away (below).

**Typed lettering is sewn one way from end to end (added 2026-11-14).** The choice above is made island by island, and a letter is an island: in a word whose strokes are 0.57 mm wide in one letter and 0.6 in the next, AVE came out as thin lines and MARIA as fat satin; and the hairlines and serifs of a small Times or Georgia (0.15 to 0.3 mm at 4 mm caps, under `satinMinStroke`) were left out of the satin layout, so a letter came out as two bars and a slash. For an object of kind `text` the engine now measures how wide its strokes are, typically (`meanStrokeWidth` in `geometry/thin.js`: each island's width, twice its area over its outline, averaged by area), once per object, in the size it is sewn at:
- at most `textLineBelow` **0.7 mm**: lines from end to end; every letter whose width is up to `textLineMax` **0.9 mm** (instead of the 0.58 of one letter) is a bean line along its skeleton;
- heavier: satin as before, and the **strips thinner than `textMinStroke` 0.4 mm are widened to 0.4** before the stitches are made (`widenThin`: the shape is opened by that width, what the opening took off is the thin part, each long narrow piece of it, at least 3 times longer than wide, is grown by what it lacks and put back; a crumb off a sharp corner is not a strip, and a shape whose every stroke is thin is left alone, because it is sewn as lines and widening it only made it heavy). The stored letters are not changed.
A bench of 168 typed texts (nine fonts, caps 3 to 15 mm, "AVE MARIA" and "Hamburgefonts 2026", the page's own text path, a render at 8 px/mm with a 0.45 mm thread, tolerant overlap against the glyph) says, against the letter-by-letter rule: texts that mix lines and satin 40 to 22, letters broken into pieces 15 to 0, texts that lose a counter of 0.5 mm or more 49 to 47, thread's ink over the letter's own 1.19 to 1.15, precision 0.896 to 0.903; mean overlap 0.863 to 0.861 (the serif and Times faces at 3 to 6 mm 0.82 to 0.805: the widened hairlines are heavier than the drawing, and are all there). What it looks like: Times bold at 4 to 6 mm reads AVE MARIA (it read "AYE MAKIA" and "\\ E MARIA"), regular sans at 5 mm is clean lines instead of lines and fat zigzag in one word. Things tried and not kept: widening every strip under 0.5 mm in every text (the regular faces' lines turned to fat satin: overlap 0.863 to 0.809), a line limit of 0.8 mm for the typical stroke (Arial Black at 3 mm, strokes 0.75, became thin lines). Not touched: lettering in a picture (the crest, a sign) goes through the tracer and the picture path, not this one; 3 mm serif caps are still too small to read.

**Thin arms** (`thinArms`): a hairline serif, a smile touching a nose, a whisker on a nose grows out of a bigger piece; the rows of a fill do not fit a stroke under 0.58 mm, so the island is opened with a disc of half `lineMaxWidth`; what the disc cannot enter, at least 1.8 mm long and 0.15 to 0.58 mm wide and meeting the piece only at its root, is cut away and sewn as a line after the body. Only pieces whose perimeter squared is at least 40 times their area and with at most 500 points are looked at.

### 7.3 Tatami fill (`fill.js`)

Parallel rows along `angle` (default 45), `spacing` 0.4 mm, stitches about `stitchLen` 3.5 mm, needle holes staggered through `stagger` 4 different shifts (the weave that gives tatami its name). Where a hole or notch interrupts a row it becomes several pieces; pieces are walked so the needle keeps moving: neighbouring rows are joined by a short stitch at the edge (the fill's own `travelMax` of 2.5 mm: a longer direct join is not made; not the planner's `travelMax` of 25 mm in 7.4), and a far next piece is reached by running along the inside of the outline (`travelRun` 14 mm, `runStep` 2.2) hidden under the border instead of cutting the thread. `pullComp` 0.2 mm is added to each row (half at each end). Shapes over 4000 points are not tried as satin, and a field cut by hundreds of holes (over 5000 points, the paper of an engraving) is sewn with cheap geometry.

**Small pieces** (`smallFill`): down to 0.3 mm² (no growth past the edge, pull compensation scaled 0.45 to 1 by width, rows stopping 0.07 to 0.15 mm inside the edge because a round 0.4 mm thread end at the edge looks 0.2 mm past it); under 40 mm² and at least 1.6 times longer than wide, rows run across the piece unless the person chose an angle.

**Fabric presets** (`presets.js`): woven (spacing 0.4, stitch 3.5, pull 0.2), knit (0.42, 3.2, 0.35, edge+tatami underlay), cap (0.38, 2.8), towel/fleece (0.45, 3.8, 0.4), leather (0.5, 3.8, 0.1, no underlay). Satin spacing is 0.3 to 0.4 (closer than a fill's rows because the holes on one edge are twice the row spacing apart). `THREAD_MM` is 0.38 for coverage checks; the preview draws 0.45 mm, so it looks about 18 % fatter than real thread.

**Underlay** (`underlay.js`): "auto" = none under 6 mm², edge walk under 40 mm² on woven fabric, edge walk plus a tatami layer above that (and for knit/cap/towel). It is a running loop inset from the edge, sewn first.

### 7.4 Moves, trims, locks (`plan.js`)

- A jump up to `trimOver` **3 mm** is just a jump; longer, the thread is tied off, **cut (TRIM)** and tied in again.
- **Hidden travel** (`hiddenTravel`, `travelMax` 25 mm, `travelStep` 2.5): a move that would lie under what is sewn *later* is stitched instead of jumped, which removed 13 % of thread cuts and 14 % of jumps on the corpus. The engine knows this from a coarse grid in which each cell remembers which item covers it last.
- Starts: a jump to a point `startLead` 0.4 mm along the run, then a stitch back onto the first point (no zero-length records), tie stitches `lockLen` 0.5 mm toward a point at least 0.6 mm along the run (a run shorter than that gets none).
- Short stitches: a stitch under `shortMm` 0.3 mm is merged into its neighbour when the path moves by at most 0.05 mm and the merged stitch is at most 7 mm; corners, tie stitches and run ends stay. Running stitches never make a hole within 0.5 mm of the previous one.
- **How each file format spells a trim** (`lib/embroidery/formats/`): DST has no trim command, so a trim is written as `trimJumps` (2 to 8, default 3) tiny jump records, which a reader turns back into a trim. JEF writes no trims at all unless the `trims` option is on (it then uses zero-length jumps; the default follows pyembroidery). EXP, VP3 and XXX have a real cut record (VP3 also ends the design with one). PES/PEC has no trim command: by the writer's own note, **every jump after the first record is written with the "trim" flag, so a Brother machine cuts the thread at each jump**, and TRIM records themselves write nothing. In a PES file the short jumps the engine left uncut (moves up to 3 mm) would therefore be cut by the machine anyway; worth checking on a real Brother.
- Checks (`checks.js`): 15 or more needle holes in a 1.5 mm square is flagged as a crowded spot; there are warnings for pieces left out, satin that was refused, etc.

## 8. Stage 4: files (`lib/embroidery/formats/`)

`writePattern(id, pattern, opts)` / `readPattern(id, bytes)`. The writers are ports of pyembroidery's (MIT); every file written in tests is read back by an independent pyembroidery. DST: 512-byte header (`LA:` label, `ST:` stitch count, `CO:` colour changes, extents), 3-byte records that move at most 121 units (12.1 mm) in each axis, so a longer move is cut into equal steps (a stitch longer than 12.1 mm becomes a jump by default: `longStitch` "jump" or "sew", and `maxStitchMm` lowers the limit), colour changes as `0xC3` records; DST carries **no thread colours** (the operator assigns threads per colour stop), whereas PES carries a thread table (the colours become the closest of Brother's fixed palette; JEF uses the closest of Janome's 78). PES/PEC records move at most 2047 units. Our six exports round-trip through an independent web reader (ThreadShifter) with the same stitches, colour changes and size (PES gains 7 lock stitches).

## 10. Known limits (be honest about these when selling it)

- **Nothing has been sewn on a real machine.** Pull compensation, density and underlay are usual starting points, not tuned to a factory's fabrics.
- **Small lettering.** Under about 4 mm cap height the counters of a, e, o close (the page warns under 3.5 mm). Typed lettering is sewn as lines when its strokes are 0.7 mm or thinner and as satin (with its serif hairlines widened to 0.4 mm) when heavier, one way for the whole text (7.2): serif faces at 4 to 6 mm are legible now, but 3 mm serif capitals are still not, and a heavy face at 5 to 6 mm is fat satin zigzag in the bench's render. A narrow-satin alternative was tried on 2026-11-13 and clogged the 3 to 4 mm sizes, so it was not kept. Lettering inside a picture (a crest, a sign: the tracer's path, not the typed one) is the weakest: the gold-on-dark school sign scores 0.74 against the source, the crest's serif capitals 0.84. Satin at junctions of non-perpendicular strokes (K, M, X) can tangle.
- **A single thread line (0.4 to 0.5 mm)** is the thinnest the engine sews; thinner source lines come out too heavy.
- **Photos and faces**: tones change in steps (about four to eight in a face), hair is a patch with a few strands, and an eye narrower than 2 mm on the cloth sews as dark marks (the page warns, and "Crop to the face" helps; from about 3 mm an eye has its white). A face under 1.5 % of the picture is not found by the person model, and a small face in a crowd not by the landmarker. Black-and-white portraits are read as artwork, so they come out with few tones (the senator in the test set: four threads); not fixed (5.8). The suit and the shirt of a portrait give colours to the face: the shirt of the rose portrait went from a vivid cyan to a lighter flat blue. A photo needs many colours (12 to 16 colour blocks, hundreds of trims), which is slow on a real machine.
- **Trims.** The engine cuts any move over 3 mm, which gave 155 to 646 trims on the eleven photo-type test pictures (median 292, against 12 to 48 for DADE, which uses plain jumps) and 83 on a simple three-colour badge made of 97 separate sewn pieces (DADE: 5). In a PES file every jump is cut by the machine anyway (see 7.4). Raising `trimOver` cuts them (5 mm: -30 %, 7 mm: -52 %, 10 mm: -70 %) at the price of loose thread floating between pieces.
- **Light shapes like the cloth** (grey pearls on a crest, white) are left as holes on the default cream cloth.
- **Colour count**: up to 12 needles by default; a photo converts to up to 16 blocks including detail threads.
- Performance: stitch generation averaged 2.3 s (7.3 s worst) on the 129-picture corpus at 100 mm; a 3,500-shape portrait went from about 3 minutes to 45 s with the same stitches; any one job is given up on after 2 minutes.

## 11. How it compared with other tools (our own test, 2026-11-12/13, not sewn)

Against DADE (png2dst.com, the strongest free rival) on 18 real Wikimedia pictures at the same physical size and default settings (DADE timed out on one, a woodcut, counted as ours winning): ours was better or level on 16 (it lost the two portrait photos at the time; the colour shares behind one of them are fixed, see 5.6, and the other was a drawing error of mine). Over the 17 pictures both tools converted, ours used about 1.7 times fewer stitches (203,591 in total against 353,168) and had a median of 2.4 % of its stitches under 0.4 mm against 48 % for DADE. It supports up to 12 colours and JPEG input (DADE: 5 colours, PNG only, 1024 px), and does not upload the picture (DADE does). Ours has more trims (median 172 against 25) and rougher bold small lettering. Thin lines (0.2 to 0.5 mm) and thin serif text are better in ours; bold 4 to 6 mm text, tiny holes and exact gaps are better in DADE's. No paid tool could be tested without accounts; independent evidence on paid tools is almost nonexistent.

**Re-run 2026-11-14, after the face work (55 new Wikimedia pictures in 15 kinds plus the 18 earlier ones; DADE converted 52 of the 55 new ones: it failed on three painted portraits, one over its free 100,000-stitch limit, two stopped as "ran much longer than expected").** On the face region (the landmark outline; both drawn on the cloth our tool chose, DADE's blocks coloured from the picture because a DST has no colours): luminance correlation with the source ours 0.943 against DADE's 0.723 on the 19 faces of the new set (ours higher on 18), 0.953 against 0.527 on the 5 faces of the earlier set (5 of 5); colour distance 8.5 against 12.5 and 8.0 against 15.4. For pictures that are not people (whole frame): ours higher on animals, cartoons, emblems, icons, line art, objects, scenes and text, level on nature. Over the 52 DADE converted, ours used 40 % fewer stitches (792,519 against 1,330,382) but **more thread cuts** (median 258 against 230). These scores reward more colours (ours 12, DADE 5) and say nothing about how a file sews; whole-frame scores are meaningless for a person picture, because ours leaves the background out. Pictures, sheets and numbers: `Documents\Kazi digitizer face tests 2026-11-14\` (not in the repo).
